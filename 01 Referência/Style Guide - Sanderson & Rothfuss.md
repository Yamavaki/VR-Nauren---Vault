# Style Guide: Learning from Sanderson & Rothfuss

> **Rule zero:** everything in this guide is a guideline, not a law. The story decides. Any rule can be broken when the scene calls for it, as long as it's on purpose and we know the effect we want. (See [[Working Agreement]].)

**Purpose:** take their *techniques*, not their *signatures*. This guide describes how they build character, feeling, mystery and worlds, and how we adapt that to short webnovel chapters and to a voice that becomes **yours**.

> Note on sources: the Sanderson PDF is *The Way of Kings **Prime***, his rough 2002 draft, not the published 2010 book. That's actually useful: it shows his strengths *and* his beginner mistakes, which he fixed later. Where his mature style is different, it's marked **(mature Sanderson)**.

---

## 1. The two masters in one table

| | **Sanderson** (the architect) | **Rothfuss** (the musician) |
|---|---|---|
| Narration | Tight 3rd person, several POVs, one POV per chapter | 1st-person memoir (older narrator about younger self) + 3rd-person frame story |
| Prose | "Window pane": clear, functional, invisible | Lyrical when it matters, plain otherwise. Rhythm and sound matter |
| Strength | Magic rules, plot structure, promises → payoff, big convergent climaxes | Voice, wit, emotion through small details, atmosphere, foreshadowing through story and song |
| Magic | Hard: rules, costs, limits, taught then exploited | Hard-ish (sympathy: links, energy, cost) + soft (names, Fae: wonder) |
| Emotion | Mostly explicit inner thought, determination under pressure | Understatement, gesture, silence, ritual |
| Weakness (for webnovel) | Info-dumps, too many POVs (in Prime), "tell then show" | Slow middles, indulgent episodes, payoffs delayed for books |

**Our blend:** *Sanderson's skeleton + Rothfuss's skin.* Plan structure, rules and payoffs like Sanderson. Write moments, voices and feelings like Rothfuss. Keep the pace of a webnovel.

## ⭐ Core principle: Show, don't tell (the default, not a law)
The story and the world should **reveal themselves organically**, through what characters do, say, notice, and choose, not through the narrator explaining. Every other section of this guide serves this rule.

**The reader should *discover*, not *be informed*.**

| Tell ❌ | Show ✅ |
|---|---|
| She was afraid of him. | She kept the table between them and never let go of her cup. |
| The city was poor and dangerous. | Children sold the same three onions all day; the guard counted his coins twice before walking past the alley. |
| Magic has a heavy cost here. | After the spell, his hand stayed cold for a week, and he stopped being able to feel his daughter's hair. |
| The empire hated the old religion. | The new coins still had a scratch where the goddess's face used to be. |
| Kael was a genius strategist. | Kael moved the salt cellar two inches, and the captain went pale. |

**How to show (the toolkit):**
- **Action and choice:** what a character *does* under pressure says who they are.
- **Body and object:** feelings go into hands, breath, voice, and the things people hold (§4).
- **Dialogue with subtext:** people rarely say what they mean. Let the reader read between the lines.
- **The POV's lens:** what a character notices first reveals them *and* the world (§3).
- **World through daily life:** prices, food, oaths, jokes, laws, what people fear. Never a history lecture (§6).
- **Consequences:** show the rule of the world by what happens when someone breaks it.
- **Trust the reader:** cut the sentence that explains what the scene already made them feel.

**When telling is right** (use it on purpose, not by accident):
- **Transitions and summary:** "Three weeks passed in the mines." Don't dramatize routine. Rothfuss skips a whole sea voyage in a few paragraphs.
- **Speed:** in fast action, a short told line keeps momentum.
- **Voice:** a 1st-person narrator's opinions *are* a form of character ("I was still terribly naive").
- **Small, needed facts:** if the reader needs one fact to follow a scene, give it in one clean line and move on.
- **Deliberate effect:** a flat, told line can hit harder than a shown scene. "My father died that winter." in the middle of an ordinary paragraph, with no description, can be devastating *because* it isn't dramatized. Shock, distance, irony and a narrator who refuses to dwell can all be done by telling.

**Rule of thumb:** *show what matters emotionally or reveals the world; tell what is only connective tissue.*

**Revision test:** highlight every emotion word (angry, sad, afraid, proud) and every "was + adjective" description. For each one, ask: can a gesture, action, object, or line of dialogue do this instead? Usually yes.

## 2. Voice: choose per story, vary per arc

You don't need one style forever. Choose a **base voice** per novel, then turn these **dials** for each arc or scene:

| Dial | Low ← → High | When high |
|---|---|---|
| Lyricism | plain ← → musical | grief, love, wonder, magic, endings of arcs |
| Interiority | outside the head ← → deep thought | decisions, guilt, plans |
| Humor | serious ← → playful | after big tension (relief), friendships, banter |
| Sentence length | short ← → long | short = action, shock. Long = calm, beauty, memory |
| Detail density | sparse ← → rich | new places, emotional anchors. Sparse in fights |
| Distance | close ← → distant/summary | summarize travel and routine; go close for key moments |

**Example plan:** Arc 1 (an orphan in the city): plain, funny, close. Arc 3 (a war): short sentences, little humor, sensory. Arc 4 (a Fae-like realm): high lyricism, strange rhythm. Same writer, different music.

### Narration options (pick one per book)
1. **Tight 3rd, 1–3 POVs** (Sanderson, mature). The easiest for webnovels. Label POV switches clearly.
2. **1st-person retrospective** (Rothfuss). An older narrator can add irony ("I was still terribly naive") and foreshadowing. **Adapt:** give the narrator a different reason to tell the story (on trial, dying, writing to a daughter, telling a god).
3. **1st-person present** (common in webnovels). Immediate but limited. Good for progression stories.

**Rule (learned from Prime's mistakes):** don't add a POV unless it has its **own engine** (goal + conflict). Six equal POVs in one book was too many. Even Sanderson cut them later.

## 3. Characters

### Build them
- **Sliders (Sanderson):** *competence / proactivity / likability.* A character can be low in one if high in another. Cold but brilliant (Jasnah); weak but kind and earnest (early Merin); good but passive (Dalenar), and the arc pushes him to *act*.
- **Want vs. need, wound vs. lie.** The first scene with a character should hint at their inner wound. Sanderson opens with Dalenar wondering what happened to the man he used to be.
- **A lens, not just a vocabulary.** Decide what each POV *notices first*: a soldier sees exits and threats, a merchant sees prices, a priest sees sin, a thief sees locks. Description becomes characterization.
- **Voice fingerprint per side character** (Rothfuss): a formal one who never uses contractions, an eager one who makes puns, a blunt one who swears. Write a one-line **voice card** for each in their character note.

### Reveal them
- **Through gesture and object:** twisting a ring that isn't there; eyes avoiding the stain on the floor where someone died; a battered case with mismatched clasps. *Tie gestures to backstory* so they can be explained later.
- **Through banter:** jab → counter-jab → **one sincere line slipped in**. The wit shows intelligence; the sincere line shows the heart.
- **Through how others react:** fear, respect, laughter. Use sparingly. Rothfuss overuses admirers praising Kvothe.
- **Let the villain win on the page** before he loses (Sanderson's Ahven). A defeated weak villain gives no satisfaction. Give the antagonist **one on-page victory per arc** and reasons that make sense to *them*.
- **Fair twists about people:** plant 2 innocent-looking clues on the character's **first appearance**. (The "idiot king" in Prime is deaf, and on his first appearance he asks someone to step into the light, because he reads lips.)

### Change them
Seed → test → costly choice:
1. **Seed** the flaw or wound in the first scene.
2. **Test** it with a mid-arc blow (a death, a betrayal, a failure).
3. **Force a choice that costs** at the climax: the character pays something real.
4. **Callback:** a mentor's line from early on becomes the tool or the pain at the end.

## 4. Emotion

The main rule: **put the feeling in the body and the objects. Name it rarely.**

| Technique | How | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Understatement | People avoid saying the terrible thing. "Bad business last night" instead of "he died horribly". The reader feels what's held back | Rothfuss |
| Physical detail | Shame = skin hot, voice cracking. Relief = a full breath. Fear = hands that won't stop moving | both |
| Ritual and repetition | Grief as a ritual: each person says one memory, then the same short line ("Everyone drank.") | Rothfuss |
| Silence / absence | What's missing: no music, an empty chair, a song not sung | Rothfuss |
| Misperception under shock | A grieving character *decides* the messenger is lying. Short sentences, denial, senses going dull | Sanderson |
| Action instead of words | When words fail, the character plays, cooks, fights, builds. The strongest move in WMF | Rothfuss |
| Honest narrator | The narrator admits the temptation to lie about a shameful act, then refuses. Builds trust. **Max 1–2× per book** | Rothfuss |
| Determination | A resolution repeated under pressure, inner monologue in italics | Sanderson |

**Avoid:** show-then-tell doubling (his face darkened… *he was angry*). Sanderson's draft does this a lot. Trust the reader.

## 5. Foreshadowing, promises & mystery

### Promises → Progress → Payoff (Sanderson)
- **Chapter 1 makes promises:** what kind of story, which mysteries, which relationships, which change. Readers are owed every promise.
- **Show progress** regularly: skills in steps, the war in clear stages, the mystery one clue at a time.
- **Pay off** in proportion: a big promise needs a big payoff. Track all of them in the **Promises list** in [[Series Outline]].

### Planting clues
- **Plant in plain sight, among normal details.** Rothfuss's inn has a sword named "Folly", brass barrel rings instead of iron, a locked chest, eyes that are oddly blue. None are explained right away, and all of them mean something.
- **The cute detail as a key.** A harmless rhyme, joke or habit early, reused at a dark moment to reveal hidden truth. (Bast's counting rhyme returns at the end and exposes a plan done off-page.)
- **Withheld recognition.** The POV feels "something familiar" but can't place it, and another character gives the answer later. **For webnovels: pay off in 2–15 chapters, not across books.**
- **Stories within stories.** Songs, legends, a children's rhyme, a holy text. They teach lore *and* prophesy. Make sure some legends are **wrong** in interesting ways.
- **The repeated line with a new meaning.** Plant one line in Arc 1 and replay it at the end, reversed. (Prime: "never met a [X] who lies" / "You just did.")
- **Bookends.** Open and close a book or arc with mirrored scenes. Change a few details to show what was lost or gained.
- **Genre signals.** A symbol that tells the reader "tragedy is coming" (Rothfuss's Cthaeh tree). Readers love learning these.

### The anomaly hook
Show something that **doesn't add up** (5,000 soldiers killed 20,000?) and let two characters notice it. One anomaly per arc is a strong engine.

### The Secrets & Reveals table is mandatory
Every secret: truth, who knows, reveal chapter, clue chapters. See [[Secrets & Reveals]].

## 6. Worldbuilding & magic

### Magic (Sanderson's Laws, plus what both books show)
1. **Your ability to solve problems with magic depends on how well the reader understands it.** Soft magic can create wonder and problems; only well-understood magic should *solve* them.
2. **Limits matter more than powers.** Costs, weaknesses, what it *can't* do. The interesting stories happen at the limits.
3. **Expand what you have** before adding new powers. Find new uses for existing rules.

**The rule chain (Sanderson):** introduce a rule in a low-stakes scene, then exploit it in higher-stakes scenes. In Prime, one small rule (a gem in the sword pommel bonds the sword) is used 4 times: humiliation in ch. 1, the explanation of a villain's sudden skill, hope in prison, self-punishment at the end. **Plan at least two later uses for every rule you introduce.**

**Cost during crisis (Rothfuss):** explain magic through what it costs *when it matters*. Kvothe's magic needs a link and heat, so in a fight he has to use his own body heat and freezes. The reader understands the rules *because* they hurt.

**Personal cost:** link the cost to the character's deepest fear (memory, family, sanity, humanity).

**Mislead with the rules:** let the reader think they know how a scene will end ("he has hot water, he'll use it as a weapon"), then resolve it differently.

### Delivering lore without info-dumps
- **Through arguments.** Two characters who know different things *disagree*. (An old student explains university politics by moving beer glasses on the table.)
- **Through a biased narrator.** A fanatic, an outsider or someone with outdated memory describes the world, so the information comes with attitude and mystery.
- **Through lessons that fail first.** The character learns, fails, understands the cost.
- **Through documents, songs, rhymes, prices.** Count money on the page. Readers believe a world with prices.
- **The iceberg:** write 10× more lore than you show.
- **Term cap:** max **3–4 new invented words per chapter** in the opening arc, each defined by use. Prime throws ~10 at the reader in chapter 1, which is too many.

### Make the world feel real
- **Metaphors from the world itself.** Draw comparisons only from things your POV knows (their trade, weather, animals, tools). Make a list of 20 everyday things in your setting and use it.
- **Ecology as reward.** After action, give a short moment of beauty that connects to feeling (flowers opening after a storm, which reminds a soldier of home).
- **A world clock.** Storms, tides, festivals, eclipses, harvest. Something that forces structure on everyone.
- **Cultural texture:** oaths, jokes, food, superstitions, money. **Use your own sources.** Brazilian folklore, Catholic-syncretic rituals, regional speech rhythms, indigenous myth, colonial history. This is how you get their *depth* without their *surface*.

## 7. Clichés: use, twist, or break

For every trope in the story, choose consciously:

| Mode | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Use straight** | Give readers the pleasure they came for, executed well | The underdog wins the tournament, but we earned it through training scenes and costs |
| **Twist** | Keep the shape, change one key element | The mentor doesn't die; he *betrays*, for reasons the MC later agrees with |
| **Subvert/break** | Set up the expectation, then deny it, and the new result must be *more* satisfying | The "chosen one" prophecy was written by the villain to control who rises |
| **Combine** | Two tropes that don't usually meet | Cultivation + a legal drama; a system apocalypse seen from a priest |

**Rules:**
- Subvert only what you've **set up properly first**. Breaking a cliché nobody noticed has no effect.
- The twist should **feel inevitable afterwards** ("of course!"), not random.
- Keep ~70% of reader expectations satisfied and ~30% surprising. Too much subversion feels like the author mocking the genre.
- Note every trope and its mode in [[Concept]].

## 8. Webnovel adaptation (how their techniques fit short chapters)

Their chapters average ~2,500 (Rothfuss) to ~3,500 (Sanderson) words. Ours are ~1,600–2,200. So:

- **One of their scenes = 1–3 of our chapters.** Cut at the moment of tension.
- **End on the reversal, not the settling.** Prime often ends chapters on a calm beat ("She had gotten what she wanted"). Move that beat to the *start* of the next chapter and end on the reveal, decision or question.
- **Punch chapters after climaxes** (Rothfuss): an 800–1,200-word aftermath chapter right after a big fight ("I woke…") shows the changed situation and gives relief.
- **Summarize travel and routine.** Rothfuss skips an entire sea voyage in one short chapter, which creates mystery for free. Never write a whole chapter where nothing changes.
- **Interludes** (a short side POV or frame chapter) as pressure valves after an emotional peak, roughly every 15–30 chapters.
- **The avalanche at arc climaxes** (Sanderson): in the last 3–6 chapters of an arc, switch between POVs or threads every few hundred words, with each section ending on a turn. **The rescue must come from a choice the hero made earlier, on the page**, never from nowhere (that's Prime's mistake; the published book fixed it).
- **Show outcomes through objects** across chapter breaks: the next chapter opens with the scarred, bloody armor, and only then do we learn who won.
- **A countable resource** (money, days, charges, a debt) updated in small mentions keeps the stakes visible in slower chapters.
- **Opening chapter essays:** a 50–150-word in-world proverb or excerpt at the top of some chapters (an invented philosopher, a holy book). It varies texture and plants themes.

## 9. Don't copy: their signatures (avoid these)

**Rothfuss signatures:** the "silence of three parts" or any "X of three parts" opening; an inn as frame story with a scribe; a red-haired, green-eyed musician-prodigy; naming as deep magic, "calling the wind"; lowercase Fae speech; a named sword over the bar; "third time pays for all"; a protagonist who is the cleverest person in every room; long seduction episodes; overused triads.

**Sanderson signatures:** swords summoned in heartbeats; Shardblade/Plate-style names; gemstones fueling magic; storms as world-defining with "Storms!" as an oath; orders named "-runners"/"-callers"; a covered left hand as modesty; an assassin bound to obey whoever holds his token; his archetype set together (honorable old general + cold scholar princess + peasant soldier + mad immortal).

**Prose tics to avoid** (both, and in Prime especially): lots of "nodded / frowned / sighed / paused"; "obviously", "slightly", "however"; adverb tags ("she said dryly"); too many em-dashes and trailing ellipses.

**Rule:** you can borrow any *one* archetype or device if you **transform its core** (its reason, its wound, its relationships). Several together = pastiche.

## 10. Avoid the "AI voice"

These patterns make text feel generated. Avoid them, or use them only with intent:
- Abstract emotion words in place of detail: *a mix of emotions, a wave of relief, a sense of dread, something shifted inside her.*
- Stock phrases: *little did he know, a testament to, couldn't help but, the weight of [abstract noun], eyes that held secrets, sent shivers down his spine, a dance of, tapestry, the air was thick with, unspoken understanding, let out a breath she didn't know she was holding.*
- Everything in threes, every paragraph ending in a neat little moral, characters always explaining their feelings in perfect complete sentences.
- Same-length sentences and same-shape paragraphs, again and again.
- Generic details (a "tavern", "a beautiful woman", "ancient ruins"). Prefer **specific, surprising, slightly odd** details (a tavern where the counter is scarred by a drunk's sword practice and nobody fixes it).
- Characters who agree too easily. Real people interrupt, mishear, change the subject, joke at bad moments.

**Fix:** you always do the final pass. Read it aloud. Add one detail only *you* would think of. Cut the sentence that explains what the reader already felt.

## 11. Chapter checklist (quick version)
- [ ] **Show, don't tell:** world and feelings revealed through action, dialogue, detail. No lectures
- [ ] First 1–3 paragraphs: who, where, what's at stake now
- [ ] One clear change (the turn)
- [ ] One specific, concrete detail of the world
- [ ] One moment of character voice (wit, gesture, lens)
- [ ] Feeling through body/object, not named
- [ ] A clue or promise planted or paid
- [ ] Max 3–4 new invented terms
- [ ] Ends on the reversal/question/decision, not the settling
- [ ] No signature copying, no AI stock phrases
- [ ] 1,600–2,200 words

## 12. Practice exercises (to build *your* voice)
1. **Lens drill:** describe the same market through a soldier, a thief, and a grieving mother (300 words each).
2. **Grief by ritual:** write a wake scene using a repeated line, without naming any emotion.
3. **Rule chain:** invent one small magic rule and write 3 tiny scenes using it at rising stakes.
4. **Banter:** 20 lines of two friends arguing over a price, ending in one sincere line.
5. **Bookend:** write an opening paragraph and its mirrored closing paragraph for an arc.
6. **Cliché three ways:** take "the mentor dies" and write it straight, twisted, and subverted (150 words each).
7. **Cut the tell:** take one of your paragraphs and remove every emotion word. Replace them with body or object.
